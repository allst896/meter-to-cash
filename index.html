Meter to cash: where billing numbers go wrong

One month of usage for three customers, followed from the meter to the payment. Each stage plants problems I've run into in real settlement work, the kind that pass the usual totals check, and then finds them with checks a reviewer can follow, down to the interval, the rate, the date and the cent.

Five stages

  1. 1

    Metering

    Which usage feed is wrong, and why?

    A feed with exactly 30 × 96 rows passes a month-level count yet comes up 332.8 kWh short. Counting intervals per day, before any cleaning, finds a six-hour outage, and keeps the repeated hour when daylight saving time ends that a naive cleanup would delete.

    Report →
  2. 2

    Rating

    Is every unit priced, in the right category, at the right rate?

    A meter in the wrong load zone, usage left unpriced after a migration, and a new engine that looks up rates by run date instead of service period. Two of the three leave every kWh priced; no single check catches all three.

    Report →
  3. 3

    Billing

    Does the statement roll forward, and is the interest right?

    Every statement ties to the cent, yet one customer is charged $1.28 too much interest: a payment received December 18 was keyed as December 28. Only an independent recalculation from the bank remittance shows it.

    Report →
  4. 4

    Invoicing

    Can the customer check the invoice themselves?

    Six sample PDF invoices, all tying to their statements. Two still fail the customer: a true-up printed as current energy, and a bare $12.97 “Adjustment” with no reference. A recompute from only what's printed finds both.

    Report →
  5. 5

    Collection

    Does each payment equal its invoice, and if not, why?

    A short-pay that equals one invoice line exactly, a check keyed to the wrong account, and a meter read that arrived a day after the cutoff. The dispute is settled on the evidence in one exchange, and aging clears.

    Report →

Follow one charge through all five

The same $12.97 shows up at every stage. It starts as a reasonable estimate and ends as a disputed short-pay, and the fix belongs at the stage where the problem began, not where it surfaced.

How it's built

  • Synthetic data for one month (November 2026, America/Chicago, 15-minute interval-ending), generated from a seed.
  • Python and pandas. The checks read the inputs, never an answer key, so they work the same on a different month.
  • The planted problems and the key figures in each report are checked by tests, run across three different random months.
  • Invoices are generated as PDFs from a fictional utility. All companies, customers and addresses are made up.
  • Source code and data: on GitHub.

About

I've worked in energy settlements for about 20 years, with experience in full-stack development, energy market analysis and EMS/SCADA systems along the way. The scenarios here come from that work: finding a daylight-saving bug by counting intervals per day, catching grouping mistakes with delta-from-normal checks, comparing systems during an upgrade, recalculating interest independently, and validating counterparty invoices against what was actually submitted. The work carries over to any business that meters usage and bills for it.
